	\section*{Application: Systematic Sampling Using Set Notation}
	
	Consider a population consisting of
	\[
	N = 45,000
	\]
	individuals. A researcher wishes to select a sample of
	\[
	n = 680
	\]
	individuals using \textbf{systematic random sampling}.
	
	The sampling interval is determined by
	\[
	k=\left\lfloor\frac{N}{n}\right\rfloor
	=\left\lfloor\frac{45,000}{680}\right\rfloor
	=66.
	\]
	
	A random starting point is selected from the first sampling interval. Suppose the researcher randomly chooses
	\[
	r=12.
	\]
	
	The first individual selected is therefore the 12th member of the population. Every 66th individual thereafter is included in the sample. The selected individuals are
	\[
	12,\;78,\;144,\;210,\;276,\;\ldots
	\]
	
	This sequence forms an \textbf{arithmetic progression} with
	\begin{itemize}
		\item First term:
		\[
		a_1=12,
		\]
		\item Common difference:
		\[
		d=66.
		\]
	\end{itemize}
	
	Hence, the \(i\)-th sampled individual is given by
	\[
	n_i=12+66(i-1),\qquad i=1,2,\ldots,680.
	\]
	
	The complete sample can therefore be expressed using set notation as
	\[
	S=\left\{12+66(i-1)\mid i=1,2,\ldots,680\right\}.
	\]
	
	Alternatively, using universal quantification,
	\[
	\forall i\in\{1,2,\ldots,680\},\qquad
	n_i=12+66(i-1).
	\]
	
	The final sampled individual is
	\[
	n_{680}
	=12+66(679)
	=44,826,
	\]
	which lies within the population of 45,000 individuals. Thus, the systematic sample consists of exactly 680 individuals selected at equal intervals throughout the population.
	
	\subsection*{Understanding the }\((i-1)\)\textbf{ Term}
	
	The reason for \((i-1)\) is that we want the first sampled person to be 12.
	
	Let's see what happens. We know the sequence is
	\[
	12,\;78,\;144,\;210,\;\ldots
	\]
	Each time we add 66.
	
	If we wrote
	\[
	n_i = 12 + 66i,
	\]
	then
	\[	i=1 \Rightarrow n_1 = 78 \quad (\times) \text{ (we wanted 12)}\]
	\[
	i=2 \Rightarrow n_2 = 144
	\]
	Everything is shifted by one.
	
	Instead, use
	\[
	n_i = 12 + 66(i-1).
	\]
	
	Now check:
	\[
	i=1:\quad n_1 = 12 + 66(1-1) = 12 + 66(0) = 12 \quad \text{Perfect.}
	\]
	\[
	i=2:\quad n_2 = 12 + 66(2-1) = 12 + 66 = 78
	\]
	\[
	i=3:\quad n_3 = 12 + 66(3-1) = 12 + 132 = 144
	\]
	Exactly the sequence we wanted.
	
	Another way to think about it is:
	
	\[
	\begin{array}{|c|c|c|}
		\hline
		\text{Sample number } i & \text{Number of jumps of 66} & \text{Selected person} \\
		\hline
		1 & 0 \text{ jumps} & 12 \\
		2 & 1 \text{ jump} & 78 \\
		3 & 2 \text{ jumps} & 144 \\
		4 & 3 \text{ jumps} & 210 \\
		\hline
	\end{array}
	\]
	
	Notice that the number of jumps is always one less than the sample number. That's why the formula uses \(i-1\).
	
	A good memory trick is:
	
	\textbf{General term of an arithmetic sequence}
	\[	a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d	\]
	where:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \(a_1\) is the first term,
		\item \(d\) is the common difference,
		\item \(n\) is the term number.
	\end{itemize}
	
	In your case:
	\[
	a_1 = 12
	\]
	\[
	d = 66
	\]
	so
	\[
	n_i = 12 + 66(i-1).
	\]
	
	This is the standard formula used in mathematics, statistics, and research methodology for arithmetic progressions.
